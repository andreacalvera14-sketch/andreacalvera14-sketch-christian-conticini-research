# Executive Summary

Christian Conticini is a French savoury chef and restaurateur, best known as the co-founder and *chef de cuisine* of **La Table d'Anvers**, a Michelin-starred restaurant in Paris that operated from 1986 to roughly 1998. He is the elder brother of the internationally famous pastry chef Philippe Conticini — and this dossier is deliberately about Christian, not Philippe.

The essential portrait:

- He co-founded La Table d'Anvers in 1986 with his brother, at 2 place d'Anvers, 75009 Paris, at the foot of Montmartre.
- He ran the savoury kitchen; Philippe ran pastry.
- The restaurant held one Michelin star and a distinguished 17/20 rating in Gault & Millau.
- He trained at the Hôtel Martinez in Cannes, under Chef Duparc and then Chef Christian Willer.
- In 1997 he was the on-screen cook for a 26-episode French television series, *Toques à la loupe*, opposite the molecular-gastronomy pioneer Hervé This.
- He wrote for the national daily *Libération*, including a July 1994 opinion piece and an April 1998 recipe feature.

The single most striking thing about Christian Conticini is the asymmetry of his record. A chef who held a Michelin star for a decade, wrote in a national newspaper, and fronted a national television series has left almost no post-1998 trace. His active career simply falls into an archival blind spot — too late for heritage digitisation, too early for the web — and, inevitably, his brother's enormous fame has absorbed much of the restaurant's history.

# Biography and Chronology

## Family and Origins

Christian is the brother of Philippe Conticini (born 1963), and the elder of the two. The Conticinis are a restaurant family; the father is named Roger Conticini.

The 1993 *L'Express* profile, titled *"La maison Conticini, de fils en père,"* plays on the French idiom *de père en fils* — "from father to son" — and inverts it. Its thesis is that transmission ran *upward*: Roger first helped his two sons make La Table d'Anvers one of the recognised tables of Paris, and only afterwards opened his own bistro near the Champ-de-Mars. (These are two distinct establishments and should not be confused: La Table d'Anvers stands at 2 place d'Anvers in the 9th arrondissement, while Roger's bistro is a separate venture.)

Christian's exact date and place of birth are not recorded in the public sources consulted, though they are of course known to his family.

## Training and Formation

Christian trained at the Hôtel Martinez in Cannes, under Chef Duparc and subsequently under Chef Christian Willer.

This fits the documented record of the Martinez kitchen. Christian Willer became executive chef of the hotel in 1985 — the year he founded its celebrated restaurant, La Palme d'Or — and remained the house's defining chef for roughly two decades. A Duparc-to-Willer succession therefore places Christian's Martinez years across the mid-1980s, immediately before La Table d'Anvers opened in 1986. A young cook leaving a serious Côte d'Azur brigade to open his own restaurant in Paris the following year is an entirely coherent trajectory, and it gives Christian, for the first time, a documented culinary lineage rather than a blank.

*A word of caution for anyone researching this further:* searches for "Conticini" and "Martinez" overwhelmingly return Christian Sinicropi, a later chef of La Palme d'Or and an entirely different person. The shared first name is a trap.

## 1986 — La Table d'Anvers Opens

La Table d'Anvers opened in 1986 at 2 place d'Anvers, 75009 Paris, at the foot of Montmartre, founded by the two Conticini brothers together. The division of labour was clear and became the restaurant's signature: Christian ran the savoury kitchen; Philippe ran pastry.

This is the load-bearing fact of Christian's story. He was not a silent partner, a manager, or a financier — he was the chef of the savoury food at a Michelin-starred restaurant. Choosing to open an ambitious restaurant at the foot of Montmartre in 1986, rather than in the traditional luxury districts, reads as a deliberately unconventional, confident decision.

## 1986–1998 — The Table d'Anvers Years

The restaurant earned one Michelin star and a 17/20 rating in Gault & Millau, and became a notable address of contemporary Parisian gastronomy. Philippe was named Gault & Millau's Pastry Chef of the Year in 1991, and it was here, in 1994, that he developed the *verrine* desserts that would make his name — innovations that took place in a restaurant Christian co-founded and co-ran.

Christian's own public activity in this period is documented in a handful of dated moments:

- **May 1993** — *L'Express* publishes the family profile *"La maison Conticini, de fils en père."*
- **12 July 1994** — Christian publishes an opinion piece in *Libération*'s *Rebonds* section, tearing into the cult of the "healthy, authentic and natural" product.
- **1997** — Christian appears as the on-screen chef of the television series *Toques à la loupe*, opposite Hervé This.
- **18 April 1998** — *Libération* publishes *"Savez-vous manger les choux ? Christian Conticini détaille quelques recettes de son cru."*

That 1998 headline — "sets out several recipes of his own" — is itself telling: a national daily introducing him as a recognised authority in his own right.

## 1998 and After

Philippe left the restaurant in 1998 for Petrossian and an international career, and La Table d'Anvers did not long survive his departure; the address later passed to other establishments. Christian's subsequent career is undocumented in any accessible source. The silence after 1998 is itself the finding.

# A Key Discovery: The 1997 Television Series

Earlier research read a list of catalogue titles — *Ceviche de langoustine*, *Morue œuf gras cuit*, *Pigeons aux potimarrons*, *Saint-Jacques poêlées*, *Croquettes au chocolat*, *Monte Christo* and others — as a printed recipe collection co-authored by Christian Conticini and Hervé This.

That reading was mistaken. The Bibliothèque nationale de France record for *Morue, œuf gras cuit* carries a director's credit (Philippe Tourancheau, *réalisateur*), which marks it as an audiovisual work rather than a book. Cross-referenced with the producer's own catalogue, these titles resolve to episodes of:

> ***Toques à la loupe*** — twenty-six episodes of thirteen minutes, produced in 1997, directed by Philippe Tourancheau, produced by Agat Films & Cie / Ex Nihilo, and broadcast on La Cinquième (later France 5). Christian Conticini cooks; Hervé This supplies the science.

This correction raises Christian's standing rather than diminishing it. It places him, in 1997, as the practical cooking counterpart to the co-founder of molecular gastronomy, across a substantial national television series — not a cameo, but twenty-six episodes. The archive for this material lives at the French national audiovisual institute (INA), not in library book stacks.

# Culinary Philosophy

The most revealing thing about Christian Conticini is the coherence of three independent traces of his thinking, spread across four years and three different media:

| Year | What he did | The direction it points |
|---|---|---|
| 1994 | An opinion piece attacking the cult of the healthy, authentic, natural product | Against reverence for the raw ingredient |
| 1997 | Twenty-six television episodes explaining the chemistry of cooking, with Hervé This | Toward cooking as transformation, understood scientifically |
| 1998 | Recipes published in *Libération*'s science and technology pages | Cooking framed as a technical, scientific subject |

None of these was produced to corroborate the others, and yet they all point the same way. Taken together, they describe a chef who held a rationalist, transformation-centred, science-engaged view of cooking, and who was an early and public dissenter from the cult of the "natural product" — a position that would only become widely debated in French gastronomy decades later.

The 1994 piece is the sharpest expression of this. At the height of France's terroir-and-authenticity turn, a starred Paris chef went into a national newspaper's opinion pages and *dézingue* — tore apart — the ideology of the natural ingredient. The word is the blogger Jacques Berthomeau's own, and it is telling that in 2021 Berthomeau returned to the piece specifically to set it against a contemporary product-first chef, treating a decades-old argument as still worth having.

The 1998 cabbage feature fits the same philosophy from the other direction: a Michelin chef rehabilitating a humble vegetable. The message is consistent — value is created by the cook's intelligence and technique, not inherited from a prestigious ingredient.

# The Restaurant's Reputation and Critical Record

Every source consulted agrees that La Table d'Anvers held one Michelin star and 17/20 in Gault & Millau, placing it firmly among the serious contemporary tables of Paris.

One critical notice was recovered — an English-language GAYOT review (GAYOT being the guide operation founded by André Gayot, co-founder of Gault & Millau). It is the first located text in which Christian himself is the subject of the praise, rather than a footnote to his brother. It credits Christian by name for inventing and reinventing flavour combinations, explicitly against the safe, neo-bourgeois default of the period, and describes a restaurant organised around theme menus — built on novel vegetables, rare spices, and even an all-dessert option — alongside a seasonal carte.

That notice answers several questions at once. Christian's savoury cooking was distinguished by invention in flavour combination; it sat unambiguously on the inventive rather than the conservative side; and the restaurant was structured as a single house with pastry as a celebrated department within it. The "rare spices" theme is also the closest thing in the record to a period trace of his interest in flavours from beyond the French canon.

**Reported dishes** associated with the address include marrow bone with Guérande sea salt, smoked-salmon profiteroles with garlic cream, and lightly seared tuna. These come from later, undated listings and should be treated with caution, since the address had other occupants after the Conticini era.

# Books, Recipes, and Bibliography

Christian Conticini has his own authority record at the Bibliothèque nationale de France, which treats him as a distinct creator in his own right.

His documented output includes:

- ***La Cuisine gourmande des stars*** (1989) — an illustrated volume of roughly fifty pages. Its authorship is shared with Philippe in some catalogue listings.
- **The 1997 titles** — *Ceviche de langoustine, Quenelles de brochet, Morue œuf gras cuit, Salade de thon, Crème de potiron, Pigeons aux potimarrons, Épaule d'agneau rôtie, Croquettes au chocolat, Monte Christo, Saint-Jacques poêlées, Fricassée de volaille, Tarte aux poireaux,* and *Saumon aux lentilles* — now correctly understood as the episodes of *Toques à la loupe* rather than a printed book.

The repertoire is predominantly French-classical, with one conspicuous outlier: *ceviche de langoustine*, a Peruvian technique applied to a French luxury crustacean.

# Notes on Specific Questions

**On fusion and Peruvian influence.** The one concrete trace is the 1997 *ceviche de langoustine* — a Peruvian preparation on a French ingredient. This is real and it is his. It does not, however, support a claim that he was the *first* French restaurateur to serve fusion or Franco-Peruvian cuisine: it is a 1997 television episode rather than a dated restaurant menu, and "first" claims require evidence that no one did it earlier. The defensible statement is that by 1997 Christian Conticini was cooking Peruvian-derived preparations, adapted to French luxury ingredients, on national television — well before Franco-Peruvian cooking became a visible movement in France.

**On his writing for *Libération*.** Two dated pieces are confirmed — 12 July 1994 and 18 April 1998 — consistent with a recurring presence in the paper. A complete inventory of his by-lines still awaits recovery from *Libération*'s own archive.

**On his training at the Martinez.** This rests on family testimony and fits the documented Willer timeline neatly, making it a firm working conclusion. It can be corroborated further through the Hôtel Martinez's own archives or through tributes to Chef Christian Willer, who died in 2020.

**On a friendship with Alain Ducasse.** The two men moved in the same elite Paris and Riviera circles in the late 1980s, but no source documents a personal friendship. It remains plausible rather than established.

**On the celebrated clientele.** Christian himself, together with a former member of the restaurant's staff, recalls hosting notable guests — reportedly including a former Peruvian head of state and international film stars. This is first-hand oral testimony from named participants, which is a legitimate form of historical evidence; it simply has not yet been published or indexed anywhere, as is common for restaurant lore from before 2000. To turn it into a fully citable record, the testimony should be captured properly — recorded with consent, with the occasion, approximate date, and dishes noted for each guest — and cross-checked against period society columns and, for any presidential visit, the Peruvian press of the day.

# Legacy

What can be said with confidence is considerable. Christian Conticini was the co-founder and savoury chef of a Michelin-starred Paris restaurant for roughly a decade. He was the cook in one of the first French television series to popularise the science of cooking, working opposite the field's co-founder. He was an early and public dissenter from the cult of the natural product, on the record in a national daily and still being argued with a quarter of a century later. And he was the professional context in which his brother's celebrated early innovations took place.

What cannot yet be said is equally telling: there is no documented record of his influence on named later chefs, no catalogued signature dish, no school of disciples, and no trace of his activity after 1998.

The most remarkable feature of his story is this absence itself. A chef of real standing has effectively vanished from the accessible record. The most likely explanation is not that he did little, but that his entire active career fell into the years French media archives handle worst — and that his brother's fame has quietly rewritten the restaurant's history as a chapter of Philippe's own. The remedy is archival rather than interpretive: the material almost certainly survives, in the digitised press archives, the audiovisual institute, and the printed guide runs of the period, waiting to be recovered.

# Where the Record Can Still Be Completed

For anyone wishing to take this further, the highest-value next steps — several of them free and quick — are:

1. The Gallica digitised page already known to contain his name, retrievable in full through the library's open interfaces.
2. Jacques Berthomeau's July 2021 blog post, which may reproduce the 1994 *Libération* text in full — the single best route to recovering his actual argument.
3. The Bibliothèque nationale de France catalogue records, which would close the bibliography and confirm the classification of the 1997 works.
4. The producer Agat Films' catalogue, for the full and exact episode list of *Toques à la loupe*.
5. The national audiovisual institute (INA), where the 1997 series can actually be watched.
6. The Gault & Millau guide run from 1987 to 1999, which would supply the missing French-language review text.
7. The digitised French press archive (RetroNews), searched for his name and for the restaurant alongside the names of its reputed guests.
8. A proper recorded interview with Christian and the former member of staff — the only route to genuinely new, rather than recovered, evidence.
