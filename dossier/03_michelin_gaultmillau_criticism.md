# 03 — Michelin, Gault & Millau, and the Critical Record

## Summary of the state of evidence

**Every source consulted agrees that La Table d'Anvers held one Michelin star and 17/20 in Gault & Millau. Not one source consulted names the guide edition, year or page.**

That gap is the whole content of this file, and it should not be papered over. A rating repeated identically across a dozen web pages, none of which cites a guide, is a rating propagating through citation rather than a rating verified. It is very probably true. It is not, on the present evidence, **[VERIFIED]** in the sense this dossier uses the word.

## §1 Primary-source facts

### The Michelin star

**Claim:** La Table d'Anvers held **one Michelin star**.
**Tag: [PARTIAL].**

**Basis:** consistently stated across multiple independent indexed sources describing the restaurant, e.g. *"Le restaurant obtint rapidement une étoile Michelin"* — "the restaurant quickly obtained a Michelin star."

**Unresolved:**
- **In which Michelin guide year does the star first appear?** The restaurant opened in 1986; the *Guide Rouge* is annual; a star awarded on the strength of a first year would appear in the 1987 or 1988 edition. **No source consulted gives a year.**
- **How long was it held?** Unknown. If it ran to the 1998 wind-down, that is roughly a decade of continuous starring — a materially different claim from a star held briefly, and it is the sort of thing that ought to be stated precisely or not at all.
- **Was it ever two stars?** No source suggests so. Recorded as "not claimed."

### The Gault & Millau score

**Claim:** **17/20** in Gault & Millau.
**Tag: [PARTIAL].**

**Basis:** stated alongside the star in multiple indexed sources, e.g. *"une note remarquable de 17/20 au Gault & Millau"* — "a remarkable score of 17/20."

**Why 17/20 is a significant number, if correct.** In the Gault & Millau system of the period, scores ran to 20 and were expressed in *toques*. 17/20 sat in the **three-toque** band — the tier below the 18–19 reserved for the small group of restaurants regarded as national summits. A 17 in the late 1980s–1990s placed a restaurant in the upper reaches of French gastronomy, and it is notably *ahead* of what one Michelin star alone would imply. Gault & Millau in this period was the guide of the *nouvelle cuisine* and of critical risk-taking, more willing than Michelin to reward invention over consistency.

**The interpretive point — and it is inference, marked as such.** The pairing of **one Michelin star with 17/20 G&M** is a recognisable signature. It is the profile of a restaurant that critics regarded as more interesting than its Michelin rank suggested: rewarded for creativity by the guide that prized creativity, held at one star by the guide that prized consistency and the trappings of luxury. For a small, inventive restaurant at the foot of Montmartre — not a palace dining room — that is exactly the pattern one would predict. **This reading is coherent and, in the compiler's judgement, likely; it rests on the ratings being correct, which is [PARTIAL].**

### Third-party standing, 1993

The L'Express article of May 1993 is reported as describing La Table d'Anvers as **"one of the recognised tables of Paris."** **[PARTIAL]** — reaching us via summary, not the page. If the phrasing is the article's own, it is the only period, non-guide, third-party assessment of the restaurant's standing located anywhere in this research.

### Philippe's 1991 award — and why it is in this file

Philippe Conticini was named **Gault & Millau Pâtissier of the Year in 1991**. **[PARTIAL]**

This is Philippe's award, not Christian's, and the brief's scope rule applies. It is recorded here for one reason only: **it dates and independently corroborates Gault & Millau's active critical attention to La Table d'Anvers in the early 1990s.** A guide does not name a house's pastry chef its pâtissier of the year without having assessed the house. It makes the existence of a G&M rating for the restaurant in that period near-certain, even though the specific number remains unconfirmed.

## §2 The critical text — one review recovered

An earlier pass through this material concluded that **no review of La Table d'Anvers, from any critic in any publication, existed anywhere reachable.** That conclusion was wrong, and it is withdrawn. A second, wider retrieval attempt — going around the blocked primary URLs rather than at them — located a **surviving guide review whose subject is Christian's cooking specifically**.

### The GAYOT notice

**Citation:** *La Table d'Anvers*, 2 place d'Anvers, 75009 Paris — restaurant notice, GAYOT (the English-language guide operation founded by **André Gayot**, co-founder of Gault & Millau). https://www.gayot.com/restaurants/la-table-danvers-paris-75009-france_10pa02200.html — now flagged "THIS RESTAURANT IS CLOSED".

**Retrieval:** **[PARTIAL]**. The page could not be opened directly — gayot.com was unreachable on the same DNS block as everything else. Its text reached this dossier through the search index, which returned it at length and consistently across repeated queries. **It is therefore reported as [SUMMARY-DERIVED] and must be confirmed against the live page before being quoted in print.**

**Rating carried on the notice: 16/20.** Note that this is **not** the 17/20 repeated across the biographical web pages. See §2.1.

**What the notice says [SUMMARY-DERIVED]:** that while so many chefs fall back on a safe, reassuring repertoire of neo-bourgeois and bistro dishes to conceal a lack of inspiration, **Christian Conticini invents and reinvents flavour combinations** with a wizardry the reviewer calls staggering; that a reader tempted by the prospect of a **real gastronomic adventure** should climb up to his Table at the foot of Montmartre; that the draw is a set of **theme menus** — built around novel vegetables, rare spices, or desserts alone — alongside a **carte keyed to the seasons**; that the options are so uniformly enticing the reviewer resorts to choosing at random; that the **desserts are by Christian's brother Philippe**; and that the cellar holds **a wealth of little-known wines**.

### Why this single document matters more than its length suggests

It is the first located text in which **Christian is the grammatical subject of the critical praise**. Everywhere else in the record, Christian appears as a dependent clause attached to Philippe. Here the structure inverts: it is *Christian's* Table, *Christian* invents, and Philippe enters as the brother who does the desserts. For a figure whose entire documentary problem is being written out of his own restaurant, that inversion is the substantive finding.

It also converts several previously unanswerable questions into answered ones:

- **What did critics say about Christian's savoury cooking?** That its distinguishing quality was **invention in flavour combination**, explicitly set against the safe neo-bourgeois/bistro default of the period.
- **Classical, modern, or inventive?** Unambiguously placed on the inventive side, and *defined by contrast* with conservatism.
- **How was the restaurant structured?** Around **theme menus** — vegetables, rare spices, all-dessert — plus a seasonal carte. The all-dessert menu is a notable early instance of dessert treated as a full savoury-equivalent course structure.
- **Was a non-French influence noted?** Not in these terms. But **"rare spices"** as an organising theme of a menu is the closest thing in the record to a period, third-party trace of the interest that later produced the 1997 *Ceviche de langoustine*. It is suggestive; it is not a statement about Peru. See §3.
- **Two brothers, one restaurant or two?** One restaurant, one authority, with pastry named as a distinct and celebrated department inside it.

### §2.1 The 16/20 problem — do not paper over this

The notice's **16/20** contradicts the **17/20** stated across the biographical pages. Both cannot describe the same edition. Three readings, none yet eliminable:

1. **Different years.** Guide scores move. A house could hold 17 at its peak and 16 in another edition, or the reverse. This is the likeliest explanation and it is unremarkable.
2. **Different guides.** GAYOT and Gault & Millau share an origin in André Gayot but have been separate operations for decades, scoring independently.
3. **The 17/20 is unsourced repetition.** It remains, as §1 records, attached to no named edition anywhere.

**Consequence for this dossier:** the 17/20 stays **[PARTIAL]**, and is now additionally flagged as **contradicted by the one rating found on an actual guide page**. A dated print edition of either guide settles it; nothing short of that does.

**A dating caution on the GAYOT page.** The indexed version of that notice carries prices in **euros**, which cannot predate 2002 — after the Conticini restaurant had gone. Guide sites routinely re-skin legacy review text with later-scraped practical data, so the review prose and the price block are probably of different dates, and the prose may well be a survival of a 1990s notice. **Do not cite the page as a dated 1990s review until the live page and, ideally, a print edition are checked.** This caveat is the reason the whole entry is [PARTIAL] and not [VERIFIED].

### What is still missing

One guide notice is not a critical record. Still not located, and still worth pursuing:

- Any **French-language** review — *Le Monde*, *Le Figaro*, *Libération*, *Le Point*, *L'Express*, *Le Quotidien de Paris*, *Thuriès Gastronomie Magazine*.
- Any **dated** Michelin or Gault & Millau entry, which alone can fix the star's first year and the true score.
- Any review that discusses the cooking's **influences** rather than its quality.

A second, unrated route now also exists: aggregator listings (Paris Gourmand, Gralon, lesrestos) carry what appear to be **period menu items** — *os à moelle à la fleur de sel de Guérande*, *profiteroles de saumon fumé à la crème d'ail*, *pavé de thon mi-cuit au saté sur fondue de poireaux*, *noix de Saint-Jacques à la compotée d'endives caramélisées*. **[UNVERIFIED]** — these listings are undated, unattributed, and may describe a later occupant of the address rather than the Conticini restaurant. **Do not treat them as Christian's dishes without confirming which régime the menu belongs to.** The *saté* on tuna, if it is his, would be a second trace of the spice-and-elsewhere interest; that is precisely why it needs checking rather than adopting.

## §3 The fusion / Franco-Peruvian question, assessed against the critical record

The brief asks whether Christian was the first French restaurateur to introduce fusion, specifically Franco-Peruvian, cuisine.

**Tag: [UNVERIFIED], and the compiler's assessment is that it is unlikely to survive scrutiny as stated.**

**What exists in favour:** an episode of *Toques à la loupe* (1997) titled ***Ceviche de langoustine***. Ceviche is unambiguously Peruvian in origin; langoustine is unambiguously French in register. The dish is a genuine Franco-Peruvian hybrid, and Christian cooked it on national television in 1997. **That much is real.**

**Why it does not establish the claim:**
1. It is a **television episode**, not a menu. It does not show the dish was served at La Table d'Anvers, or when, or for how long.
2. It is **1997** — eleven years after the restaurant opened. It cannot support a claim about the mid-1980s.
3. **"First" claims require negative evidence** — a demonstration that nobody did it earlier. No such survey exists here. Franco-Peruvian fusion is conventionally dated later in France (the Gastón Acurio era, post-2000), but "conventionally dated later" cuts both ways: it makes a 1997 instance *early and interesting*, and it makes a 1986 "first" harder to credit without documentation.

**A defensible reformulation.** The evidence supports: *"By 1997, Christian Conticini was cooking Peruvian-derived preparations such as ceviche, adapted to French luxury ingredients, on national television — well before Franco-Peruvian cuisine became visible in France in the 2000s."* That statement is supportable, specific and still striking. The "first French restaurateur" formulation is not supportable and should be retired unless a period menu or review is produced.

**What would settle it:** a dated **menu** from La Table d'Anvers, or a period review naming Peruvian or Latin-American dishes. See `07_open_questions_and_leads.md`.

## §4 How to verify the ratings — concrete, executable steps

These are written for a human with an unrestricted browser and, where noted, a library card.

### Michelin

1. **The guides themselves.** The *Guide Michelin France* is published annually and held by the BnF and by most large French municipal libraries. Consult the **1987 through 1999** editions, Paris 9e section, address **2 place d'Anvers**. This is the only fully authoritative route and it is a physical-consultation task. Record: first year of the star, every year present, year of disappearance.
2. **BnF catalogue** — search `Guide Michelin France 1987` etc. to identify shelf marks before travelling.
3. Note that Michelin does **not** publish a public historical database of past ratings, so no online shortcut exists.

### Gault & Millau

1. **The annual guides**, same approach, editions **1987–1999**. Confirm the 17/20 and note the year(s). The written G&M notice will also be a **review** — solving the §2 gap at the same time. This is the single most efficient physical-archive action available.
2. **Gault & Millau magazine** back issues for the same period.

### Press reviews

Use RetroNews and the newspapers' own archives with the queries set out in `07_open_questions_and_leads.md`.
