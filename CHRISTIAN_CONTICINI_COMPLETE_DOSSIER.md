## 1. Executive summary

Christian Conticini is a French **savoury chef and restaurateur**, best known as the **co-founder and chef de cuisine of La Table d'Anvers**, a Michelin-starred restaurant in Paris that operated from 1986 to roughly 1998. He is the **elder brother** of the internationally famous pastry chef **Philippe Conticini** — and this document is deliberately about Christian, not Philippe.

The core, reasonably solid picture:

- He **co-founded La Table d'Anvers in 1986** with his brother, at 2 place d'Anvers, 75009 Paris, at the foot of Montmartre. **[VERIFIED]**
- He ran the **savoury kitchen**; Philippe ran pastry. **[VERIFIED]**
- The restaurant held **one Michelin star** and **17/20 in Gault & Millau** (widely repeated, though one recovered guide page shows 16/20). **[PARTIAL]**
- He **trained at the Hôtel Martinez, Cannes**, under Chef Duparc and then Chef Christian Willer. **[FAMILY TESTIMONY]**
- In **1997** he was the on-screen cook for a 26-episode French TV series, ***Toques à la loupe***, opposite the molecular-gastronomy pioneer **Hervé This**. **[PARTIAL, strongly supported]**
- He wrote for **Libération**, including a **12 July 1994** op-ed and an **18 April 1998** recipe piece. **[PARTIAL]**

The single most striking thing about him is the **asymmetry of the record**: a chef who held a star for a decade, wrote in a national daily, and fronted a national TV series has almost no post-1998 digital footprint. Most likely his active career (1986–1998) simply sits in the archival "dead zone" — too late for heritage digitisation, too early for the web.

## 2. Biography and chronology

### 2.1 Family and origins

- Christian is the **brother of Philippe Conticini** (b. 1963). **[VERIFIED]**
- Christian is the **elder** of the two. **[FAMILY TESTIMONY]** — this settles a point that every web source leaves open.
- The family is a **restaurant family**; the father is named **Roger Conticini**. **[PARTIAL]** (from the indexed summary of the L'Express May 1993 article).
- The L'Express piece, *"La maison Conticini, de fils en père,"* inverts the idiom *de père en fils* ("from father to son"): its thesis is that transmission ran **upward** — Roger first helped his two sons make La Table d'Anvers a recognised Paris table, and only afterwards opened his own bistro near the Champ-de-Mars. **[PARTIAL]**
  - **Do not confuse the addresses:** La Table d'Anvers is at 2 place d'Anvers (9th arrondissement). Roger's bistro is a separate establishment near the Champ-de-Mars.

**Not established:** Christian's exact date and place of birth (Philippe's 1963 is documented; Christian's is not recorded in any consulted source — though it is of course known to his family).

### 2.2 Training and formation (pre-1986)

Christian trained at the **Hôtel Martinez, Cannes**, under **Chef Duparc**, then under **Chef Christian Willer**. **[FAMILY TESTIMONY]**

Why this fits the documented record:
- **Christian Willer** became executive chef of the Hôtel Martinez in **1985**, founding its restaurant **La Palme d'Or** that year, and remained the house's defining chef for about two decades. **[VERIFIED, on Willer]**
- A **Duparc → Willer** succession therefore places Christian's Martinez period across the **mid-1980s**, immediately before La Table d'Anvers opened in 1986. A young chef leaving a serious Côte d'Azur brigade to open his own Paris restaurant the following year is an entirely coherent path.
- This gives Christian, for the first time, a documented **culinary lineage** rather than a blank.

**Name-collision warning:** searches for "Conticini" + "Martinez" overwhelmingly return **Christian *Sinicropi*** (a later Palme d'Or chef — a different person). The shared forename is a trap; do not treat Sinicropi material as Conticini material.

### 2.3 1986 — La Table d'Anvers opens

- Opened **1986** (the month of May is reported but only **[PARTIAL]**) at **2 place d'Anvers, 75009 Paris**. **[VERIFIED for year and location]**
- Founded by **both brothers together**. **[VERIFIED]**
- **Division of labour: Christian = savoury kitchen; Philippe = pastry.** **[VERIFIED]** — this is the load-bearing fact of the whole story. Christian was not a financier or manager; he was *the chef of the savoury food* at a starred restaurant.

*Inference:* opening at the foot of Montmartre in 1986 — not a luxury-gastronomy district — reads as a deliberately unconventional, ambitious choice.

### 2.4 1986–1998 — the Table d'Anvers years

- **One Michelin star; 17/20 Gault & Millau.** **[PARTIAL]** — universally repeated, never sourced to a specific guide edition.
- Became a **notable address of contemporary Parisian gastronomy.** **[PARTIAL]**
- **Philippe named Gault & Millau Pâtissier of the Year, 1991** (context only — it dates the restaurant's peak visibility and confirms G&M was critically engaged with the house). **[PARTIAL]**
- **Verrine desserts dated to 1994** at the restaurant, credited to Philippe (context only). **[PARTIAL]**

## 3. The single most important finding: the 1997 "recipe series" is a television series

Earlier research (including ChatGPT's) read a list of BnF work-titles — *Ceviche de langoustine*, *Morue œuf gras cuit*, *Pigeons aux potimarrons*, *Saint-Jacques poêlées*, *Croquettes au chocolat*, *Monte Christo*, and others — as a **printed recipe collection** co-authored by Christian Conticini and Hervé This.

**That was an error.** The BnF notice for *Morue, œuf gras cuit* (`ark:/12148/cb444579018`) carries a **`Philippe Tourancheau réal.`** credit — *réalisateur*, i.e. **director**. A record with a director is an **audiovisual record**, not a book. BnF classes these as *image animée* (moving image).

Cross-referenced with the producer's own catalogue, these titles resolve to episodes of:

> ***Toques à la loupe*** — 26 × 13 min, 1997, directed by **Philippe Tourancheau**, produced by **Agat Films & Cie / Ex Nihilo**, broadcast on **La Cinquième** (later France 5), with **Christian Conticini** cooking and **Hervé This** supplying the science.

This correction *raises* Christian's standing: it places him, in 1997, as the practical cooking counterpart to **the co-founder of molecular gastronomy**, across a substantial 26-episode national television series — not a cameo. The real archive for this material is at **INA / the INAthèque**, not in library book stacks. **[PARTIAL→strong]**

## 4. Culinary philosophy

We possess **not one sentence of Christian's own prose in original form**, so this section is the most inferential — but it rests on a genuinely strong evidential move: the **convergence of three independent traces**.

| Year | Trace | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| 1994 | Op-ed *dézingue* ("tears apart") the cult of the healthy/authentic/natural product | Against reverence for the raw ingredient |
| 1997 | 26 TV episodes explaining the chemistry of cooking, with Hervé This | For cooking-as-transformation, understood scientifically |
| 1998 | Recipes published in Libération's **science/technology** ("futurs") section | Cooking framed as a technical, scientific subject |

Three different media, three institutions, four years, **one direction** — and none produced to corroborate the others. That convergence is real evidence.

**The reasonable conclusion, at proper strength:** Christian Conticini held, in the 1990s, a **rationalist, transformation-centred, science-engaged** view of cooking, and was an **early public dissenter from the cult of the "natural product"** — a position that would only become widely debated in French gastronomy decades later (the debate Berthomeau explicitly revives in 2021, setting Christian against contemporary product-first chef **Bruno Verjus**).

- The one word we can attribute to the source itself is **« dézingue »** (it is in the blog post's own URL) — strong, colloquial French for "to tear apart / demolish." Berthomeau's word, not a paraphrase.
- The 1998 cabbage piece fits the same pattern: a starred chef rehabilitating a humble vegetable — value created by the cook, not inherited from a prestigious ingredient.

## 5. Michelin, Gault & Millau, and the critical record

**Every consulted source agrees on 1 Michelin star + 17/20 Gault & Millau. Not one names the guide edition, year, or page.** So the rating is almost certainly correct but remains **[PARTIAL]** — repeated identically across pages, none of which cites a guide.

- **One review was recovered** — an English-language **GAYOT** notice (GAYOT was founded by André Gayot, co-founder of Gault & Millau). It is the **first located text in which Christian is the grammatical subject of the praise**: it credits **Christian by name** for *inventing and reinventing flavour combinations*, explicitly against the "safe neo-bourgeois/bistro default" of the period, and describes **theme menus** built on novel vegetables, rare spices, and an all-dessert option. **[PARTIAL]**
- **A 16/20 discrepancy:** that GAYOT notice carries **16/20**, not 17. Possible explanations: different years, different guides (GAYOT ≠ Gault & Millau today), or the 17/20 being unsourced repetition. Flagged, not resolved.
- **Dating caution:** the indexed GAYOT page shows prices in euros (impossible before 2002), so its practical data post-dates the Conticini era even if its prose is period.

**Reported menu items** (from aggregators, undated, **[UNVERIFIED]** — treat with suspicion as the address had later occupants): *os à moelle à la fleur de sel de Guérande*; *profiteroles de saumon fumé à la crème d'ail*; *pavé de thon mi-cuit au saté*.

## 8. Legacy — and the shape of its absence

**What can be claimed:**
1. **Co-founder and savoury chef of a Michelin-starred Paris restaurant** for ~a decade (1986–c.1998).
2. **The cook in one of the first French TV series to popularise molecular gastronomy**, opposite the field's co-founder — the most durable claim available.
3. **An early public dissenter from the cult of the natural product** (1994, still argued with in 2021).
4. **The professional context in which Philippe's early career happened** — including the 1994 verrines, which occurred at a restaurant Christian co-founded and co-ran.

**What cannot be claimed:** any named later influence, signature dish, school/disciples, or post-1998 activity — no sources.

**The absence itself** is best explained by **archival timing** (his whole active career sits in the badly-digitised 1986–1998 window), compounded by **Philippe's fame absorbing the restaurant's history**. The corrective is archival, not interpretive.
